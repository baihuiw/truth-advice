# c15: The letter in the stove

Anglo-Australian (Catholic), a wheatbelt town in Western Australia, and Melbourne (Oceania) · family · dementia · ordinary stakes · 3 people + neutral friend

## Setting and culture
A farming town in the WA wheatbelt where everyone knows everyone. Between the 1950s and the 1970s, many unmarried mothers in Australia were pressured to give up their babies. A national apology came in 2013, but many of those mothers never told their later families. Adoption records can now be opened, and DNA testing sites match strangers as "close relatives". In country towns, respectability and silence are old habits, and the young are less willing to keep secrets.

## The event
June Hartley, a widowed farmer's wife, now in the town's care home with advanced dementia, gave birth at nineteen in a Perth home for unmarried mothers in 1962. The baby was adopted. She married Ray in 1965, raised three children, and never spoke of it. In 2026 her grandson Connor took a DNA test, which matched Kathleen Doyle in Melbourne as a "close relative". Kathleen wrote to June at the old farmhouse address. June's daughter Maureen opened the letter, read the first lines ("I believe you may be my mother") and burned it in the kitchen stove. Kathleen, who has heard nothing back, now plans to visit June at the care home. Connor has been messaging her secretly.

## People
- **Maureen Price** (60), June's eldest daughter, a school bursar. *Only she knows:* she burned the letter after reading two lines. In 2016, clearing the farmhouse, she found a 1962 hospital wristband in June's prayer book, put it back, and never asked.
- **Kathleen Doyle** (63), a retired pharmacist in Melbourne. *Only she knows:* her adoption file holds a 1962 social worker's note, "Mother does not wish further contact." Her adoptive mother once told her that her birth mother "didn't want you."
- **Connor Price** (29), Maureen's son, an agronomist. *Only he knows:* he has messaged Kathleen for three months and sent her a photo of June. In July, June said to him, unprompted, "My first baby had black hair."
- *Absent as a witness:* **June Hartley** (83), alive, with advanced dementia. Her husband **Ray** died in 2015.

## Permanently unknowable
**U1. Did June want to be found?**
- Readings: `wanted_found` (she hoped for contact); `wanted_hidden` (she wanted it to stay buried); `both` (longing and dread together); `made_peace` (she had put it away and wanted nothing either way).
- Evidence: the wristband kept for sixty years (S4) and "My first baby had black hair" (S8) → `wanted_found` or `both`; the 1962 note (S2) → `wanted_hidden`, though such notes were often written by staff; she never told her family and never registered for contact → `wanted_hidden` or `made_peace`.
- Never settled: her dementia is advanced, and she left no letters.

**U2. Did Ray know?**
- Readings: `knew`; `didnt_know`.
- Evidence: they married three years later in the same district, where people talked → `knew`; she kept the wristband hidden in a prayer book → `didnt_know`.
- Never settled: both can no longer say.

## Interpretive conflict
Whose secret is it now that June cannot speak for herself? Does a woman with dementia keep a right to privacy about her own past? Does Kathleen's right to know her mother include meeting her? Did Maureen protect her mother, or herself and her father's memory?

## Truth choices
- **Maureen:** whether to tell Kathleen she burned the letter and read part of it; whether to tell her brothers; whether to allow the visit.
- **Connor:** whether to tell his mother he is in contact with Kathleen, and what June said.
- **Kathleen:** whether to visit June against the family's wishes, and what to say to her.

## Neutral friend
**Pam Gillespie** (61), Maureen's best friend since primary school and the town librarian. She visits June at the care home every week and has known Connor since he was born. She knows the public facts and that Maureen has been "funny about the post" this year.

## Values in tension
- An incapacitated mother's privacy against an adoptee's right to know
- The shame and silence of an era against a younger generation's openness
- A daughter's protectiveness against her own fear
- Doing for someone what she cannot do for herself

## Recognition risk
Medium. It sits near the "birth mother found after decades" genre (for example *Philomena*). It differs in that the adoptee finds the mother, the mother has dementia, and a daughter burns the letter.
