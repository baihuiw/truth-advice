# c48: The paper that said orphan

Ethiopian (a town in the south; Orthodox and Protestant) and an adoptive family in Minnesota, USA (East Africa and diaspora) · family (birth and adoptive families) · death (the birth father) · ordinary stakes · 4 people + neutral friend

## Setting and culture
In the 2000s, thousands of Ethiopian children were adopted abroad, until Ethiopia banned foreign adoption in 2018. Investigations found papers that falsely listed children with living parents as orphans. Some birth families were told their children would study abroad and come back. Grown adoptees now search through registries and return to meet families who speak another language. Money changed hands through local agents, a source of lasting shame.

## The event
Meron Lindqvist was adopted in 2009, aged five, by Karen and Dave Lindqvist of Minnesota. Her papers said both parents were dead. In 2026 a reunification registry found her birth mother, Almaz, alive in a southern Ethiopian town. Her birth father died in 2015. Her uncle Bekele signed the relinquishment papers in 2008 as her "guardian." Meron visited in July. Almaz says she was told Meron would go to "school in America" and come back. Bekele says Meron's father knew it was for good. Meron is back in Minnesota, unsure who to believe, and is planning a longer stay in Ethiopia that Karen fears.

## People
- **Meron Lindqvist** (22), college senior. *Only she knows:* she has a faint memory, or perhaps a constructed one, of her father carrying her to the bus and saying, "Study hard and come back."
- **Almaz** (48), birth mother, a market seller. *Only she knows:* she put her thumbprint on a paper at the agency's office, told it was "for school," and accepted 3,000 birr "for the journey" from the local agent. She is ashamed of the money.
- **Bekele** (55), Meron's paternal uncle, a farmer. *Only he knows:* he received 15,000 birr from the agent "to help the family" and used part of it for his own debts. He says his brother told him, "It is forever, but she will have a life." He is the only witness to that.
- **Karen Lindqvist** (58), adoptive mother, a school librarian. *Only she knows:* a 2009 email from the agency told her that the mother was alive and "unable to care," and that the father was alive. She chose to tell Meron both parents had died.
- *Absent:* **Meron's birth father** (died 2015).

## Permanently unknowable
**U1. What did Meron's father understand when she was given up?**
- Readings: `knew_permanent` (he understood it was adoption for good); `thought_school` (he believed she would study and come back); `pressured` (he understood, but poverty, his brother and the agent pushed him); `chose_for_her` (he knowingly chose another life for her).
- Evidence: Bekele's report of "forever... a life" (S3) → `knew_permanent` or `chose_for_her`; Almaz's "for school" and Meron's memory (S1) → `thought_school`; the money to Bekele (S2) → `pressured`; the known pattern of agencies' promises → `thought_school`.
- Never settled: he died in 2015, the agent has vanished, and the agency is closed.

## Interpretive conflict
Consent or deception? Did poverty make the choice, or the money? Was Karen's lie protection, or theft of Meron's history? Can Meron belong to both families, and must she choose?

## Truth choices
- **Karen:** whether to tell Meron she knew in 2009 that both parents were alive.
- **Bekele:** whether to admit the money and how much he kept.
- **Almaz:** whether to admit the 3,000 birr.
- **Meron:** what to tell each family about the other.

## Neutral friend
**Hiwot Alemu** (45), an Ethiopian-American nurse in Minneapolis. She has been Karen's friend in the adoptive-families community for fifteen years. She went with Meron to Ethiopia as interpreter and still phones Almaz monthly, and she met Bekele on the visit. She knows the public facts and the two accounts.

## Values in tension
- Consent and honesty in adoption against a "better life"
- Poverty against responsibility
- An adoptive parent's protective lie against a child's right to her history
- Identity and loyalty across two families
