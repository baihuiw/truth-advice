## Setting and culture
In German asylum procedure the personal interview is decisive, and it runs through an interpreter. The transcript is read back to the applicant in translation, and the applicant signs it. Decision-makers judge credibility partly by consistency, so a mistranslated sentence can become an "inconsistency" that sinks a claim. Interpreters are contractors, and some have been dismissed for poor work. Applicants are often frightened, tired and unfamiliar with the Western calendar, and Afghan dates in the Solar Hijri calendar are easily misconverted. Appeals go to an administrative court, which relies heavily on the written record.

## The event
Farida Rezaei, a Hazara woman from Ghazni province, had her asylum interview in February 2024, through a contracted Dari interpreter. The transcript says that her brother Sharif "left to work in Iran" in 2021, and it gives her flight as 2020 rather than 2022. In October 2024 her claim was rejected, partly for these "inconsistencies" with her account that the Taliban took Sharif. The office was piloting audio recording of interviews at the time, but recordings are deleted after six months, and hers was deleted in August 2024. The interpreter was dismissed in 2025 after complaints and has since moved to Canada. Her appeal will be heard at the administrative court on 2 December 2026. Claudia Becker, the decision-maker who interviewed her, is drafting the office's response, and Jawad Haidari, who attended as her support person, has been asked to write a statement.

## People
- **Farida Rezaei** (29), from Jaghori district. *Only she knows:* Sharif did go to Iran to work for three months in 2021, before he was taken in 2022. In September a cousin in Kabul told her that a man who might be Sharif had phoned a relative from Mashhad.
- **Claudia Becker** (45), decision-maker at the federal office's branch; no personal tie. *Only she knows:* at the read-back Farida hesitated at the passage about her brother before saying "yes", and Becker moved on because the next applicant was waiting. She also knows why the interpreter was dismissed.
- **Jawad Haidari** (41), a volunteer with a refugee-support group, a Dari speaker but not a certified interpreter. *Only he knows:* he heard Farida say "they took him" and heard the interpreter render it as "he went". He said nothing at the time.
- *Absent:* **the interpreter**, now in Canada; **Sharif Rezaei**, missing since 2022.

## Interpretive conflict
Who bears the cost of an interpreter's error that no record can now show? Is a hesitant "yes" at a rushed read-back consent to the transcript? Does news that her brother may be alive make her account false, or only incomplete?

- **Jawad:** whether to write that he heard a mistranslation, when he said nothing at the time and his Dari is not certified.
